# Kettlebell-only training

**Question:** What are the benefits of training with only kettlebells, a pull-up bar and a dip bar, instead of dumbbells, barbells and gym machines?

**Setup:** Strictly kettlebells, plus a pull-up bar and a dip bar. No dumbbells, barbells or machines.

**Researched:** 26 November 2026

## Verdict

A kettlebell, pull-up bar and dip bar is a legitimate, compact, full-body setup for general strength, power, conditioning, grip and mobility. It is not a like-for-like replacement for barbells or machines if the goal is maximal strength, fine progressive overload, or targeted muscle isolation.

The evidence base is thin. The broadest review, covering 99 publications to 2019, rates effects as typically trivial to small, with low to very low confidence. Most trials are small and last 6 to 12 weeks.

## What works well

### Strength and power

Kettlebells improve strength and power, but the evidence does not show they are superior to other tools.

- Lake and Lauder (2012): 21 healthy men, aged 18 to 27, doing swings twice a week for 6 weeks. Half-squat strength rose 9.8% and jump height rose 19.8%, with no significant difference from jump-squat training.
- A 2015 systematic review of 5 studies, ages 18 to 72, found improvements in bench press, back extension, clean-and-jerk and explosive strength. It concluded there is moderate evidence that kettlebells may be safe and effective for increasing certain functional strength and power measures in young, healthy populations.
- Otto et al. (2012): 30 healthy men, 2 sessions a week for 6 weeks. Both kettlebell and weightlifting groups gained strength and power, but weightlifting gained more strength. Neither group changed body composition.

### Grip

Grip is the strongest, most consistent finding.

- The BELL trial (Meigh et al., 2022): 32 insufficiently active adults aged 59 to 79, 12 weeks of supervised hardstyle training, 3 times a week. Grip strength rose 7.1 kg on the right and 6.3 kg on the left. One-rep-max deadlift rose 16.2 kg, 30-second sit-to-stand rose 3.3 reps, 6-minute walk rose 41.7 m, appendicular lean mass rose 0.65 kg, and resting heart rate fell 7.4 bpm. Attendance was 91.5%.
- A 2018 trial in 33 women aged 65 to 75 with sarcopenia, training twice a week for 8 weeks, also found higher grip and back strength than the control group.
- The mechanism is plausible: the thick handle and offset centre of mass below the handle force continuous forearm, grip and stabiliser work, especially in swings, cleans, snatches and carries.

### Conditioning

This is often overstated.

- The 2015 systematic review found no effect on aerobic endurance as measured by VO2max.
- Jay et al. (2011): 40 workers, 3 times a week for 8 weeks. Aerobic fitness did not change, despite strength and pain improvements.
- Smaller, shorter studies do show high heart rates during swings, and one found a small VO2max gain in NCAA soccer players after 4 weeks of snatch intervals. That makes kettlebells plausible for conditioning, but not proven to improve aerobic fitness better than, or instead of, dedicated aerobic training.
- The widely quoted ACE figure of 272 kcal in a 20-minute snatch workout comes from one very hard workout in 10 experienced users, with no comparison group. Do not use it as a general fat-loss claim.
- The BELL trial did improve 6-minute walk distance by 41.7 m in inactive older adults. That is a functional-endurance gain, not a VO2max gain.

### Core and posterior chain

The biomechanics are strong, the outcome evidence is weaker.

- McGill and Marshall (2012): 7 trained men, 16 kg two-handed swing. Gluteal activity was about 80% of maximum voluntary contraction, low-back extensors about 50%, and L4-L5 compression about 3,200 N, a moderate spinal load, well under a heavy deadlift.
- Andersen et al. (2016): 16 men, 16 kg. One-arm swings produced greater contralateral upper erector-spinae activation; two-arm swings produced 40 to 59% higher rectus-abdominis activation than one-arm swings. EMG activation is not proof of superior core-strength gains.
- The offset load also creates anti-rotation and anti-lateral-flexion demand in unilateral presses, rows, carries and get-ups. That is a real mechanical difference from a dumbbell, whose centre of mass sits in the palm, and from machines, which follow a fixed path. Whether that translates into better real-world core strength than other training is unproven in head-to-head trials.

### Mobility, balance and pain

- Jay et al. (2011): neck and shoulder pain fell 2.1 points and low-back pain fell 1.4 points, on a 0 to 10 scale, versus control, plus higher trunk-extensor strength, in 40 workers after 8 weeks.
- The 2015 review found improved postural control in one study.
- The Turkish get-up is described in expert and ACE/NASM guidance as requiring hip mobility, thoracic mobility, lumbar stability and a stable shoulder platform. That is expert opinion and coaching consensus, not trial evidence that it improves mobility more than other training.
- The single pain trial does not show that kettlebells treat back or neck pain in general.

## What the pull-up bar and dip bar add

Kettlebells cover hinge, squat, horizontal pull, vertical push and carries well. They cover two patterns poorly.

- **Heavy vertical pulling.** A kettlebell row is a horizontal pull, limited by what one arm can row and stabilise. A pull-up loads the lats, biceps, upper back and rear delts with full bodyweight through a vertical pull, and grip is trained in a dead hang. Chin-ups, underhand, bias the biceps more.
- **Heavy horizontal pushing.** A kettlebell press is vertical. There is no good heavy horizontal press with a kettlebell; the floor press is awkward and load-limited. Parallel-bar dips load chest, anterior deltoid and triceps with bodyweight. Leaning forward biases chest, staying upright biases triceps.
- **Hanging core work.** Hanging knee and leg raises and L-sits add anti-swing core and hip-flexor loading that complements kettlebell core work.
- **A complete upper-body push and pull pair.** Pull-ups and dips together train all major upper-body muscle groups, the classic calisthenics pairing.

Beginners often cannot do one strict pull-up or dip, so the bars only add this with assisted progressions: dead hangs, slow negatives, or band assistance. Progressive overload beyond bodyweight needs a dip belt, vest or added weight, which is outside the strict three-item setup.

## Where it falls short

| Area | Kettlebell plus bars | Dumbbells, barbells and machines | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maximal strength | Load capped by bell size, commonly topping out around 48 kg, and by what you can clean and rack | Barbell loads to hundreds of kg in small increments | Barbell clearly superior |
| Progressive overload | Coarse, typically 4 kg jumps. Progression must come via reps, sets, tempo, unilateral variations and density | Barbell plates allow 1.25 to 2.5 kg jumps; machines use small pin-stack steps | Barbell, machine and dumbbell superior for precise, long-term overload |
| Leg development | Goblet and front squats, lunges, split squats, step-ups, single-leg RDLs, swings and deadlifts build quads, glutes and hamstrings, especially if sets are taken close to failure. Unilateral work roughly halves the load needed per leg | Barbell back and front squat, leg press and hack squat load legs far heavier without grip or arm fatigue as the bottleneck | Adequate for general leg size and strength; capped at the top end. Once you can goblet-squat around 24 to 32 kg for 10 to 20 reps, arms, grip and upper back fatigue before legs |
| Muscle isolation | Almost none. No curl, lateral raise, leg extension or curl, or calf raise equivalent that loads the target muscle in isolation | Dumbbells allow curls, raises, flyes and kickbacks; machines isolate a single muscle and joint with a fixed path | Machines and dumbbells clearly superior for isolation, lagging muscles and rehab |
| Chest | Dips cover it at bodyweight; no heavy bench-press equivalent | Barbell and dumbbell bench press, machine chest press, flyes | Gap partly closed by dips; ceiling is bodyweight unless weight is added |
| Back, horizontal pull | One-arm rows are good but load-limited | Barbell and dumbbell rows, cable rows, lat pulldown | Pull-up bar closes the vertical-pull gap; horizontal-pull ceiling remains lower |
| Hypertrophy overall | Possible if sets are taken close to failure, but reaching failure in legs and back is harder when grip or rack position fails first | Same hypertrophy principles, easier to apply, as load can be matched to the target muscle without a weak link | Comparable for beginners and intermediates; barbells and machines pull ahead for advanced hypertrophy |
| Evidence base | No long-term comparative trial of kettlebell versus dumbbell versus barbell exists | Barbell, dumbbell and machine training has a far larger, longer-term evidence base for strength and hypertrophy | The kettlebell evidence base itself is the gap |

Other gaps: calves, neck, forearms in isolation, rear delts and lateral delts are poorly served. There is no calf raise, shrug, lateral raise or rear-delt equivalent that loads these directly. Carries and rows help forearms and traps indirectly.

## Safety and learning curve

- The 2015 systematic review reported no adverse events in any of its 5 included studies.
- The 2019 scoping review found no clear or quantifiable risk of harm, but noted it is unclear if the absence of reported adverse events is a true representation or a limitation in reporting. It included 3 case reports of injury, and noted anecdotal delayed-onset muscle soreness, bruising and forearm discomfort from repetitive impact in novices.
- The BELL trial had 4 non-serious adverse events across 12 weeks in 32 adults aged 59 to 79: 1 low-back pain, 1 shoulder pain, and 2 intercostal pain, mostly resolving. There were no serious events.
- Grinds, such as deadlift, goblet squat, press, row and carry, are learnable by a beginner from day one with a light bell. Ballistics, such as swing, clean and snatch, need the hip hinge first and benefit from coaching. A badly timed swing loads the lower back instead of the hips.
- Beginner guidance generally suggests starting at 8 kg for women and 12 kg for men, learning the hinge for 1 to 2 weeks, in the order deadlift, goblet squat, carry, then swing, and learning the Turkish get-up unloaded before adding weight.
- Specific hazards: the offset centre of mass plus momentum, wrist and forearm bruising from a poor clean and rack, and lower-back strain from squatting the swing instead of hinging.

## Who it suits best

**Best for:** general-population adults wanting full-body strength, conditioning, grip and mobility in little space and time and on a small budget; home trainees; older and inactive adults under supervision; athletes wanting hip power and posterior-chain work as a complement; people who enjoy skill-based, ballistic training and will learn technique.

**Less suited, or needs modification:** competitive powerlifters, Olympic lifters and bodybuilders chasing maximal load or isolation; people with no access to coaching who want to jump straight to snatches or heavy get-ups; anyone with shoulder, wrist or back pain or a medical condition, who should ask a doctor or physiotherapist first; people who cannot yet do a pull-up or dip, who will need assisted progressions before the bars add value; very tall or heavy trainees, who may outgrow available bell weights for legs sooner.

## Whole-body menu

| Pattern | Kettlebell | Bar |
|---|---|---|
| Hinge and posterior chain | Two-hand swing, one-arm swing, Romanian deadlift, kettlebell deadlift | — |
| Squat and legs | Goblet squat, double front squat, Bulgarian split squat, lunge, step-up, single-leg RDL | — |
| Vertical pull | — | Pull-up, chin-up, dead hang, slow negatives, band-assisted |
| Horizontal pull | One-arm row, double row | Australian, or inverted, row under a low bar, if available |
| Vertical push | Strict press, push press, Turkish get-up | Dip, also horizontal push, pike push-up |
| Horizontal push | Floor press, awkward and light | Parallel-bar dip, push-up |
| Carry, core and grip | Farmer's carry, rack carry, bottoms-up carry, suitcase carry | Hanging leg raise, L-sit, dead hang |
| Full-body and mobility | Turkish get-up, clean and press, snatch, advanced and coached only | Muscle-up, advanced only |

**Starter session, 3 days a week, expert guidance not trial prescription:** deadlift 3 x 10, goblet squat 3 x 10, swing 3 x 12, press 3 x 8 per side, row 3 x 10 per side, plus pull-ups and dips as ability allows. Progress by adding a rep per set every 2 to 3 weeks, then moving up a bell size when all sets are completed cleanly with 1 to 2 reps in reserve. Learn the deadlift before the swing. For the Turkish get-up, use 1 to 3 reps per side, slow, quality over load.

## What we still do not know

- There is no head-to-head long-term trial of kettlebell-only versus dumbbell versus barbell versus machine programmes for strength and hypertrophy.
- Optimal kettlebell loads for maximising power and strength are unknown.
- Turkish get-up benefits for shoulder stability, mobility and injury resilience are coaching and expert claims. No randomised trial was found testing the get-up alone against a control for those outcomes.
- Calorie-burn and fat-loss claims rest on a single small, uncontrolled study in experienced users. No trial shows kettlebells burn fat better than other exercise.
- Whether the BELL trial gains generalise to unsupervised home training is untested. All BELL training was supervised.
